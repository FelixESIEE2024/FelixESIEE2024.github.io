Research Question

Do AI-generated videos exhibit geometric inconsistencies that can be used to distinguish them from authentic videos?

Traditional methods mainly look for digital artifacts related to generation, compression, or pixel statistics. However, these traces become less visible as generative models improve. This work explores another approach: checking whether a video respects the physical and geometric constraints of a three-dimensional scene.

Animation

Abstract

The approach estimates depth and camera motion, then reconstructs each video frame from the previous one. The difference between the reconstructed frame and the frame actually observed provides a photometric error, which is used as a measure of geometric coherence.

The results support the hypothesis. In a controlled setting, the metric detects artificial deformations and generally distinguishes a generated video from its real-video reference. However, for unpaired scenes, the error depends too strongly on content, motion, and acquisition conditions to support reliable general classification.

Metric

The comparison is based on the Mean Absolute Photometric Error (MAE). For each valid pixel, we compare the intensity in the frame actually observed with that in the frame reconstructed through geometric reprojection.

N denotes the set of pixels that can be compared reliably. Pixels projected outside the frame or hidden by occlusion are excluded using the validity mask and z-buffer. A low error indicates that the estimated motion and geometry explain the next frame correctly; a high error signals a possible inconsistency.

Method

1. Estimation

Estimate depth, camera parameters, and relative pose.

2. Reconstruction

Transform image pixels into 3D points.

3. Reprojection

Project the reconstructed points into the next frame.

4. Comparison

Compute the difference between the predicted and observed frames.

Depth maps are obtained with Depth Anything. Intrinsic parameters are estimated with VGGT, selected for its temporal stability. Relative pose is calculated through geometric optimization, and occlusions are handled with a z-buffer. Only pixels considered valid contribute to the final calculation.

Dataset Creation

Its contents are summarized below:

Real Videos (Co3D)

The real videos come from the Co3D dataset. They provide references for measuring pipeline performance on authentic scenes.

Artificially Distorted Real Videos (TPS)

This category is created by locally distorting one frame from a real video with a Thin-Plate Spline transformation. These controlled sequences verify that the metric responds correctly to a known geometric inconsistency.

Videos Generated from Pairs (Luma AI)

To create meaningful comparisons, the first and last frames of a real video are provided to Luma AI’s Dream Machine. The model generates the intermediate sequence while reproducing the same scene and similar camera motion.

Examples of Paired Videos

The real and generated videos depict scenes that are as similar as possible. No result is displayed for the first example.

Experimental Study

1. Selecting Intrinsic Parameters

Camera parameters should remain constant in the videos studied, which do not include zoom. Across 100 real videos, VGGT provides much more stable estimates than GeoCalib: the mean standard deviation is 9.44 pixels for VGGT, compared with 705 pixels for GeoCalib. Temporal averaging greatly improves GeoCalib, but VGGT retains the lowest reprojection error and is selected for the remaining experiments.

Method Mean Absolute Error
VGGT 5.288
Averaged VGGT 5.224
GeoCalib 9.297
Averaged GeoCalib 5.397

2. Detecting an Artificially Modified Frame

Before applying our metric to the detection of AI-generated videos, we tested its sensitivity on real videos containing an artificial geometric distortion (TPS). In 100% of cases, the distorted frame has a higher reprojection error than the other frames, confirming the metric’s ability to detect local inconsistencies.

In addition, across a subset of 100 videos, the modified frame is correctly identified in 73% of cases, compared with 71.59% for GeCo.

These results validate our approach before it is applied to AI-generated videos. The experiment also shows that the method effectively detects localized distortions, even when they are difficult to see. Finally, a local analysis of the metric could improve deepfake detection, since such alterations are often limited to regions such as the face.

3. Comparing Paired Real and Generated Videos

In 27 of the 33 pairs, the generated video has a higher photometric error than the real video. On average, the error rises from 5.894 for real videos to 8.944 for generated videos. When both sequences depict the same scene, some of the content-related variability is neutralized.

For the bench sequence, the mean error is 7.359 for the real video and 12.058 for the generated video. The difference is present across much of the sequence and is not based on a single frame.

An error heatmap was also created. It highlights the areas of the frame where the error is highest and clearly identifies inconsistent regions in the scene.

4. Results across the Full Dataset

When videos are no longer compared in pairs, the result is much less clear. The mean error reaches 5.90 for real videos, 6.16 for generated videos, and 9.0 for artificially distorted videos.

Group Mean Error
Real videos 5.90
AI-generated videos 6.16
Artificially modified videos 9,0

The distributions of real and generated videos overlap. A threshold set between 5.90 and 6.16 would therefore produce many false positives and false negatives. A complex real video may receive a higher error than a simple generated video. The score reflects not only the video’s origin, but also texture, motion speed, occlusions, lighting, and the overall complexity of the scene.

The metric is therefore more robust for relative comparisons between two similar videos than for making an absolute decision about an arbitrary video.

5. Supervised Classification

To avoid setting a threshold manually, the features extracted by the pipeline are fed into an ExtraTrees classifier. The resulting accuracy is approximately 60%. This result is slightly above chance for a balanced binary problem, but remains insufficient to validate reliable detection.

The current features remain highly scene-dependent, and several rely on the same depth and camera estimates. Combining them therefore introduces some redundancy. Adding GeCo outputs does not significantly improve this overall classification. Under the current protocol, the hypothesis that a simple classifier could automatically learn a general threshold is therefore not validated.

6. Sensitivity to Acquisition Conditions and Prompting

Across 30 videos, reducing the frame rate causes an average error increase of 26%. A lower frame rate increases the apparent displacement between two frames and makes depth estimation, pose estimation, and reprojection more difficult. Compared videos must therefore exhibit similar motion speeds.

A limited study also shows that the prompt influences geometric coherence. In 8 out of 10 cases, adding an explicit instruction to preserve geometric coherence reduces the error. However, the number of trials remains too small to establish a general effect.

Analysis and Limitations

To our knowledge, this work provides the first rigorous experimental demonstration that AI-generated videos exhibit measurable geometric inconsistencies. The hypothesis is not supported solely by a few visual observations: it is evaluated using a controlled protocol, an explicit metric, and reproducible quantitative results. The method locates an artificial distortion in 73% of the sequences and assigns a higher error to the generated video in 27 of the 33 pairs studied.

These two independent experiments show that the reprojection error does not behave randomly: when a real video and a generated video depict the same scene, the generated version is generally more difficult to explain using coherent depth, camera parameters, and rigid motion. The project therefore establishes an initial quantitative protocol that turns an intuition—that video models do not always respect 3D geometry—into an observable and measurable physical phenomenon.

The study also precisely identifies the conditions under which this result is valid. The evidence is strong for paired comparisons and rigid scenes, but it does not yet constitute a universal detector: in unpaired scenes, motion, occlusions, and reconstruction difficulty also influence the score. This limitation directly motivates future work: normalizing the metric according to scene dynamics, integrating semantic information, and exploiting other physical constraints such as lines, planes, normals, shadows, or 3D trajectories.
